# How to use this guide

> *This handbook is a staged route through French grammar from first contact (A1) to highly flexible, stylistically controlled use (C2). Read the level you are studying in order, then use the appendices as a quick-reference system.*

The Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR) describes what learners can do; it does not impose one universal list of grammar points for every course or exam. The level placement used here is therefore a practical learning sequence. Some schools introduce a structure earlier or later. What matters is the depth of control expected at each stage.

## What changes from A1 to C2

| Level | Main grammar aim | What successful control looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | Build simple clauses in the present | Give basic information, ask questions, negate, describe, locate and express immediate plans |
| A2 | Link present, past and future | Tell short stories, use object pronouns, compare and express routine needs with growing accuracy |
| B1 | Organise connected discourse | Narrate with several tenses, express hypotheses, report speech and use the high-frequency subjunctive |
| B2 | Handle complex clauses and contrasts | Select mood and tense accurately, compress information, qualify claims and shift emphasis |
| C1 | Control perspective, cohesion and register | Make fine choices among competing structures and write dense, natural extended discourse |
| C2 | Exploit the full system stylistically | Manipulate ambiguity, rhythm, literary forms, idiom and register with near-native flexibility |

## A repeatable way to study every unit

1. Read the **learning goal** and the formation rule.
2. Say each French example aloud and notice what changed from the English structure.
3. Cover the translation and reconstruct the meaning.
4. Complete the **check yourself** prompt before reading the answer.
5. Write three personal examples: one affirmative, one negative and one question whenever the structure permits it.
6. Recycle the structure in a short paragraph or voice note the following day.

> **Usage note:** Recognition is not mastery. At A1-A2, aim to produce the form accurately in short sentences. At B1-B2, aim to choose it while speaking or writing connected text. At C1-C2, aim to choose among alternatives for nuance, rhythm and register.

## Conventions used in the handbook

- French examples are shown in bold, followed by an English translation.
- An asterisk before a form means it is ungrammatical in standard French.
- **Formal** normally refers to careful writing or respectful speech. **Informal** refers to ordinary conversation among familiar people.
- Parentheses show an optional element; a slash separates alternatives.
- The symbol **V** means a verb in the required form; **infinitive** means the dictionary form, such as *parler*, *finir* or *prendre*.

# Foundations: how French sentences work

> *French is largely a subject-verb-object language, but agreement, articles, clitic pronouns and verb tense carry information that English often expresses differently. These foundations make the later levels much easier.*

## The basic clause

> **Pattern:** **Subject + conjugated verb + complement**: *Lina lit le journal.* A finite French clause normally states its subject, even when the English translation could omit or weaken it.

- The **subject** performs or experiences the action: *Lina*.
- The **conjugated verb** changes for person, tense and mood: *lit*.
- A **complement** completes the idea: a direct object, indirect object, adjective, place or other information.

> **Example**
>
> **French:** Lina lit le journal.
>
> *English: Lina reads the newspaper.*

> **Example**
>
> **French:** Nous sommes prêts.
>
> *English: We are ready.*

> **Example**
>
> **French:** Il pleut.
>
> *English: It is raining.*

> **Watch out:** French normally requires an impersonal subject in weather and similar expressions: *Il pleut*, *Il faut partir*, *Il est tard*. Do not translate this *il* as a specific male person.

## Agreement: the recurring principle

Many French forms agree with another word in **gender** (masculine/feminine) and **number** (singular/plural). You will meet agreement in articles, adjectives, some pronouns and past participles.

> **Pattern:** masculine singular *un ami français*; feminine singular *une amie française*; masculine/mixed plural *des amis français*; feminine plural *des amies françaises*.

> **Example**
>
> **French:** Cette petite maison est blanche.
>
> *English: This small house is white.*

> **Example**
>
> **French:** Ces petits appartements sont blancs.
>
> *English: These small apartments are white.*

> **Usage note:** Spoken French often hides written agreement because final consonants are silent. Build the habit of checking agreement separately when writing.

## Verb anatomy

An infinitive usually ends in **-er**, **-ir** or **-re**. To conjugate many verbs, remove the infinitive ending to find a stem, then add an ending that matches the subject. Irregular verbs change the stem, the endings or both.

> **Example**
>
> **French:** parler -> je parle, nous parlons
>
> *English: to speak -> I speak, we speak*

> **Example**
>
> **French:** finir -> je finis, nous finissons
>
> *English: to finish -> I finish, we finish*

> **Example**
>
> **French:** prendre -> je prends, nous prenons
>
> *English: to take -> I take, we take*

Compound tenses use an **auxiliary** (*avoir* or *être*) plus a past participle: *j'ai parlé*, *elle est partie*. Mood shows the speaker's stance: indicative for presented facts, subjunctive for many non-asserted or evaluated ideas, conditional for hypotheses and reported futures, and imperative for commands.

## Elision, apostrophes and contractions

French avoids certain adjacent vowel sounds. Short words ending in a mute vowel often elide before a vowel or mute *h*: *je aime* becomes *j'aime*; *le hôtel* becomes *l'hôtel*. Aspirated *h* blocks elision: *le héros*, *la honte*.

> **Example**
>
> **French:** J'habite à Lyon.
>
> *English: I live in Lyon.*

> **Example**
>
> **French:** L'amie d'Alex arrive.
>
> *English: Alex's friend is arriving.*

> **Example**
>
> **French:** Le héros parle à la journaliste.
>
> *English: The hero speaks to the journalist.*

The prepositions *à* and *de* contract with *le* and *les*: *au, aux, du, des*. They do not contract with *la* or *l'*. The resulting *des* can mean either **some** or **of/from the**, depending on structure.

## Spoken and written French

Standard written French retains *ne* in negation, uses more inversion and favours explicit connectors. Everyday speech frequently drops *ne*, uses *on* for *nous*, and prefers intonation or *est-ce que* for questions.

> **Example**
>
> **French:** Je ne sais pas. / Je sais pas.
>
> *English: I do not know. (standard / informal speech)*

> **Example**
>
> **French:** Nous allons partir. / On va partir.
>
> *English: We are going to leave. (formal-neutral / conversational)*

> **Usage note:** Learn the complete standard structure first. Then learn which parts are regularly reduced in conversation; this prevents informal habits from causing errors in careful writing.
