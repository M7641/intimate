# When everything gets easier, the easy things multiply

AI changes the economics of doing. A task that once cost an afternoon now costs a prompt. Drafting, summarising, refactoring, researching, prototyping, designing — the friction that used to stand between an idea and its execution has thinned to almost nothing. This is, by any honest measure, a gift. More can be done by fewer people in less time at lower cost.

But a gift like this has a shadow, and the shadow is easy to miss precisely because the gift is so welcome. When doing becomes cheap, the discipline that used to be enforced by cost disappears. Scarcity is a filter. When you could only do three things this week, the constraint did some of your thinking for you — it forced a ranking, made you confront trade-offs, and quietly killed the marginal ideas before they cost you anything. Remove the constraint, and the filter goes with it. Now you can do thirty things, and so the temptation is simply to do them — to act on whatever seems useful in the moment, because the moment is cheap and the action is easy.

The trouble is that "seems useful in the moment" and "builds long-term advantage" are not the same set, and they overlap less than we like to think. Plenty of work is locally sensible and strategically empty: the tidy refactor that no one needed, the dashboard no one will read, the feature that polishes a corner of the product no customer cares about, the report generated because it could be generated. None of it is wrong, exactly. Each piece is defensible on its own terms. That is what makes it dangerous. A day full of reasonable, useful-feeling tasks can leave you no closer to anything that matters, and AI makes such days easier to assemble than ever before.

## Motion is not progress

There is an old confusion between activity and achievement, and AI pours fuel on it. The faster you can produce output, the more output becomes the thing you measure — and the more output you measure, the more it feels like progress. But output is not the same as advantage. Shipping ten things that don't compound is worth less than shipping one thing that does. A strategic advantage is, almost by definition, something that accrues: it builds on itself, it is hard for others to copy, and it changes your position rather than merely occupying your time. Most of what is easy to do with AI does none of these things. It is motion, and motion is seductive because it is visible and immediate and feels like the opposite of laziness.

The deeper risk is that AI lowers the cost of doing without lowering the cost of doing the *wrong* thing. In fact, it raises the latter, in relative terms. When work was expensive, a misplaced priority cost you one unit of effort. When work is cheap, you can pour ten units in the wrong direction before you even notice, because nothing pushes back. The feedback that scarcity used to provide — the felt weight of an hour spent — has gone quiet. You can be enormously, efficiently productive in service of nothing in particular, and feel good the whole time.

## The scarce skill is no longer execution

If execution is no longer the bottleneck, then the bottleneck has moved to judgment. Specifically, to prioritisation: the ability to decide what is worth doing at all, and in what order, and what to refuse. This was always a valuable skill. What is new is that it is now almost the *only* scarce one in the chain. When anyone can build anything quickly, the advantage no longer lies in building. It lies in knowing what to build — and, just as importantly, in having the discipline to leave the rest undone.

This is harder than it sounds, because prioritisation is an act of subtraction, and subtraction is unnatural when addition is free. Saying no to a task you could trivially complete feels like leaving value on the table. It isn't — the value of focus is the value of everything you didn't do, crowding out the one thing that mattered — but it doesn't feel that way in the moment, and the moment is where the decision gets made. So prioritisation in the age of AI is partly a skill and partly a temperament: the willingness to sit with the discomfort of capacity unused, to treat "I could" as a question rather than an answer, and to keep asking whether a thing seems useful or actually moves you forward.

The practical response is to make prioritisation deliberate, because it will no longer happen on its own. Decide what the genuine strategic objectives are before the week fills up with cheap, plausible tasks. Hold them somewhere visible and measure work against them rather than against the satisfying sensation of having done a lot. Ask, of each appealing thing, not "is this useful?" — almost everything clears that bar — but "does this compound toward something I've decided matters?" And practise the refusal itself, treating it as the core competence it has become, because the constraint that used to do this work for you has been automated away, and nothing has volunteered to replace it.

AI has handed us an enormous lever. A lever multiplies whatever force you apply, in whatever direction you point it — including the wrong one. The force is now abundant. The direction has become sovereign.
