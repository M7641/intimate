# Listening, pronunciation, speaking and corrective feedback

Part of the [Parle pedagogy survey](README.md).

## 1. Pronunciation: what to target

**Intelligibility, not nativeness.** Munro & Derwing (1995) established that
accentedness, comprehensibility (perceived effort) and intelligibility (actual
understanding) are related but partially independent: speech can be heavily
accented yet fully intelligible. Levis (2005, 2020) formalised this as the
**Intelligibility Principle** versus the **Nativeness Principle**. Munro &
Derwing's 2020 "redux" reaffirms the distinction after 25 years.

**Functional load prioritisation.** Munro & Derwing (2006): high-functional-load
consonant errors substantially degraded comprehensibility; low-FL errors had
minimal effect. Small exploratory study; a 2023 lingua-franca replication found
the principle only partially predictive.

**Instruction works, but the effect shrinks as measures get more realistic.**
Lee, Jang & Plonsky (2015), 86 reports: *d* = 0.89 (within-group) and *d* =
0.80 (between-group). Larger effects for longer interventions, treatments
providing feedback, and more controlled outcome measures. Saito & Plonsky
(2019), 77 studies: instruction is most effective when it targets specific
features measured under controlled conditions; effects on globally-rated,
spontaneous speech are much less clear. **This is the single most important
caveat in the pronunciation literature.**

**HVPT.** Logan, Lively & Pisoni (1991) trained Japanese learners on /r/–/l/
with multiple talkers, minimal pairs, forced-choice identification and
trial-by-trial feedback: 78.1% → 85.9% perception accuracy over 15 sessions.
Bradlow et al. (1997, 1999) showed transfer to production without production
training, retained at 3 months.

The modern meta-analytic picture is more nuanced:

- **Perception** (Uchihara, Karas & Thomson, 2025; 79 studies): *g* = 0.92
  within-participant (0.71 corrected for publication bias), *g* = 0.67
  between-participant, *g* = 0.98 retention. Identification tasks beat
  discrimination; keyword response labels beat images; benefit accrues up to
  ~400 minutes of training then plateaus. Generalisation to novel stimuli was
  negligible.
- **Production** (Uchihara, Karas & Thomson, 2024; 31 studies): *g* = 0.49
  within, *g* = 0.66 between, but only 4.5% gain on untrained items, *g* = 0.26
  retention, and a **participant-level perception–production correlation of
  only r = .09**. Adding explicit phonetic information roughly doubled
  production gains (*g* = 0.94 vs 0.45); ~20–30 min per session was optimal.

**Design implications.** Build HVPT as a core module: multi-talker,
minimal-pair, two-alternative identification with immediate feedback,
word-level keyword labels, ~20–30 min sessions, ~6–7 hours total per contrast
set before diminishing returns. Select contrasts by functional load for the
learner's L1. Do not assume perception training alone fixes production; pair it
with explicit articulatory explanation and production practice. Measure at
least some outcomes in spontaneous speech.

**Segmentals vs suprasegmentals.** Contested. Prosody-focused instruction tends
to transfer better to spontaneous comprehensibility; segmentals matter for
lexical intelligibility. Consensus is a mix. The CAPT literature overwhelmingly
targets segmentals (Amrate & Tsai 2024), a gap not a verdict.

**Age.** Hartshorne, Tenenbaum & Pinker (2018), n = 669,498, found
grammar-learning ability preserved to ~17.4 years then declining. Van der Slik
et al. (2022) reanalysed the same data and found a continuous decline fits
better for most learner types. Flege & Bohn's **revised Speech Learning Model
(SLM-r, 2021)** rejects a maturational cutoff for speech: phonetic categories
remain updatable across the lifespan; outcomes are driven by input quality and
quantity. **Do not build an adult programme on the premise that native-like
pronunciation is impossible, but set intelligibility, not accent elimination,
as the stated goal.**

## 2. Shadowing, repetition, read-aloud

Kadota's and Hamada's account: shadowing loads phonological working memory and
forces attention to the acoustic signal, training bottom-up perception. Hamada
(2016) found it benefits lower-proficiency learners more, mainly at the
phoneme-perception and word-recognition level.

A 2025 systematic review of shadowing for pronunciation (44 studies) found
improvements in comprehensibility, fluency and prosody, but **inconclusive
results for segmental accuracy**; fluency was the most consistently positive
outcome. Study quality is generally weak.

**Design implications.** Use shadowing as a bottom-up decoding and prosody
trainer, not as a comprehension activity. Sequence: comprehension check →
shadowing → repeat. Best positioned early-to-mid proficiency.

## 3. Listening

**Bottom-up decoding.** Field (2008) argues the dominant "comprehension
approach" tests listening rather than teaching it. His process approach
diagnoses breakdowns at decoding, lexical segmentation and parsing, and drills
them directly: connected speech phenomena (elision, assimilation, weak forms)
being the chief culprits.

**Metacognition.** Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari (2010), 14 weeks, metacognitive
cycle (plan → first listen → verify → second listen → reflect): the treatment
group significantly outperformed controls, and less-skilled listeners gained
most.

**Captions.** Montero Perez et al. (2013) meta-analysis: L2 captions *g* = 0.99
for listening comprehension (15 studies) and *g* = 0.87 for vocabulary (10
studies). On L1 subtitles vs L2 captions the evidence diverges: Vanderplank
suggests L1 subtitles suit beginners and L2 captions higher levels; more recent
work finds L2 captions most reliably support form noticing.

**Speed.** Slower rates help low-proficiency learners with segmentation, but
artificially slowed audio strips out the elisions and reductions of natural
speech and can disrupt prosodic parsing cues.

**Extensive listening.** Chang & Millett (2014, 2016) compared reading-only,
reading-while-listening, and listening-only across ten graded readers over 13
weeks: **reading-while-listening produced the most consistent gains**.

**Design implications.** Three separate listening tracks: (a) intensive decoding
drills on connected speech with transcript reveal and dictation-style gap
checks; (b) a metacognitive cycle around every extended listen; (c) extensive
and narrow listening at high volume. Prefer natural-rate audio with pause and
repeat-segment controls over pitch-corrected slowdown; if offered, cap
slowdown (~0.8×) and default back to natural rate. Default to L2 captions with
a delayed-reveal option, reserving L1 subtitles for beginners and gist.
Reading-while-listening is the cheapest, best-evidenced input mode you can
ship.

## 4. Speaking and fluency

**Levelt's model as adapted by de Bot (1992) and Kormos (2006):**
conceptualisation → formulation → articulation → self-monitoring. The key L2
difference is that formulation and articulation are not automatised, so
processing runs serially, consuming attention and starving monitoring. Fluency
work is proceduralisation work.

**4/3/2 (Nation, 1989):** same monologue delivered to three successive
listeners in 4, then 3, then 2 minutes. **Task repetition** (Bygate 2001):
reliable fluency gains; complexity and accuracy gains mixed.

**Skehan's Trade-off Hypothesis** versus **Robinson's Cognition Hypothesis**:
Skehan predicts learners cannot maximise complexity, accuracy and fluency
simultaneously. Meta-analytic evidence is split. Trade-offs tend to dissipate
across repetitions.

**Design implications.** Make repetition first-class: same task, three
deliveries, decreasing time, changing audience (a chatbot can supply the fresh
audience cheaply). Offer pre-task planning time as a switchable variable. A
"speak fast" task and a "speak precisely" task should be separate exercises
with separate scoring.

## 5. Corrective feedback

| Meta-analysis | Scope | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Russell & Spada (2006) | 15 studies | mean *d* = 1.16 overall; written CF *d* = 1.31; authors caution about small k |
| Li (2010) | 33 studies | medium overall effect, durable; implicit feedback better maintained than explicit; lab > classroom; FL contexts *d* = 0.74 vs L2 contexts *d* = 0.41 |
| Lyster & Saito (2010) | 15 classroom studies, N = 827 | significant, durable effects; **prompts > recasts**; clearest on free-constructed-response measures |

**Recasts vs prompts is a live dispute.** Goo & Mackey (2013) argue the
comparison is confounded ("prompts" bundles several feedback types; modified
output opportunities differ). Their position is that the two work
synergistically.

**Timing.** Unresolved. Several studies favour immediate feedback for grammar;
others find immediate and delayed equally effective.

**Written CF.** Truscott (1996–2021) argued grammar correction is ineffective;
Ferris, Bitchener & Knoch, and Ellis et al. report that groups receiving
correction outperformed no-feedback controls. Fair summary: focused CF on a
small number of treatable forms shows durable gains; unfocused comprehensive
correction has weaker support.

**Design implications.** Default to **prompts/elicitation** for
known-but-unstable forms and **recasts** for genuinely new forms and
pronunciation. Vary; do not standardise on one type. Deliver most feedback
immediately on form-focused tasks and after the turn on fluency tasks, and let
the learner set that switch. For written feedback, pick 2–3 target forms per
cycle. Expect classroom-realistic effects nearer *d* ≈ 0.4–0.7.

## 6. Affect: willingness to communicate and anxiety

MacIntyre et al. (1998) model L2 **Willingness to Communicate** as a situated
pyramid: momentary readiness to speak is shaped by state self-confidence and
desire to communicate. Horwitz, Horwitz & Cope (1986) established Foreign
Language Classroom Anxiety. The two are consistently negatively correlated.

**Design implications.** Reduce fear of negative evaluation: private practice,
no public ranking, retry without penalty, feedback framed on comprehensibility
rather than accent. Raise state self-confidence before each speaking act via
planning time and rehearsal, which is also what fluency research prescribes.
Give the learner control over feedback intrusiveness.

## 7. Conversation partners: humans, tutors, bots

**Tutoring.** Bloom's (1984) "2 sigma" claim has never replicated at scale.
Cohen, Kulik & Kulik (1982) found *d* ≈ 0.33; a 2020 meta-analysis of 96
tutoring studies averaged **d ≈ 0.37**. **Do not use 2σ as a design benchmark.**

**Tandem/telecollaboration.** Mostly qualitative outcomes: intercultural
competence, confidence, reduced anxiety. Hard learning-gain evidence is thin.

**AI chatbots.** Two 2025 meta-analyses converge on a medium effect: Wang,
Cheung, Neitzel & Chai (2025), 70 effect sizes from 28 studies, **g = 0.484**;
Lyu, Lai & Guo (2025), 31 studies, **g = 0.608**. Many comparisons are against
no-practice controls. Gains in fluency, WTC and reduced anxiety are attributed
to judgement-free, always-available practice. Short interventions, novelty
effects, and pre-LLM chatbots mixed with LLM ones.

**ASR/CAPT.** Amrate & Tsai (2024), 30 studies: CAPT generally improves
pronunciation, most clearly at beginner and intermediate levels. But systems
"still struggle to accurately evaluate L2 speech," producing **erroneous
feedback on speech that is perfectly comprehensible**, and feedback is often
uninterpretable. Current tools are poor at diagnosing which mispronunciation
occurred.

**Design implications.** An AI partner is defensible for fluency, WTC and
anxiety, and is the practical answer to the tandem-scheduling problem, but
should be positioned as high-volume practice, not a tutor substitute; the
honest benchmark is *g* ≈ 0.5. For ASR pronunciation feedback: score at the
level the model is reliable at (utterance-level comprehensibility, high-FL
contrast discrimination) rather than confident phoneme-level verdicts; never
flag an error you cannot explain; prefer perception checks (did the system
understand you?) over articulatory verdicts.

## Points of genuine disagreement

1. Does perceptual training transfer to production? Bradlow et al. yes;
   Uchihara et al. (2024) largely no at the individual level.
2. Recasts vs prompts.
3. Feedback timing.
4. Written CF (Truscott vs Ferris/Bitchener), converging only on focused CF.
5. Critical period for accent.
6. L1 subtitles vs L2 captions.
7. Trade-off vs Cognition Hypothesis for task design.
8. Segmentals vs suprasegmentals priority.
9. Slowed vs natural-rate audio.
