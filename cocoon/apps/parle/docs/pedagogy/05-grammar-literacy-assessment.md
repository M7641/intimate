# Grammar, literacy, scripts, assessment and adaptivity

Part of the [Parle pedagogy survey](README.md).

## 1. Grammar: sequences, teachability, and how to sequence for self-study

**Developmental sequences are real but narrower than usually claimed.**
Processability Theory (Pienemann) posits an implicational hierarchy of
processing procedures, and its Teachability Hypothesis holds that instruction
can only promote a stage-bound structure when the learner is at the immediately
preceding stage. But PT constrains only a small set of processing-dependent
phenomena (word order, agreement across phrase boundaries). It says nothing
about lexis, most function words, or most of what a course actually teaches.

**The "natural order" is an emergent product of item properties.**
Goldschneider & DeKeyser's (2001) meta-analysis showed that five item-level
determinants (perceptual salience, semantic complexity, morphophonological
regularity, syntactic category, frequency) jointly account for the large
majority of variance in acquisition order (~71% from a composite salience
factor). The order is predictable from features of the form, not a
maturational program.

**Explicit instruction beats implicit instruction, including for complex
forms.** Norris & Ortega (2000): explicit *d* ≈ 1.13 vs implicit ≈ 0.54. Spada
& Tomita (2010) found the explicit advantage held for complex as well as simple
features, and on free-production measures.

**Input flooding and input enhancement are weak.** Lee & Huang (2008), 16
studies: visual input enhancement beat unenhanced but flooded text by only *d*
= 0.22, with a negative *d* = −0.26 on meaning comprehension.

**Structured input (Processing Instruction) works, but the mechanism is
contested.** PI consistently equals or beats output-based traditional
instruction on comprehension while matching it on production. Replications on
complex targets often find no PI advantage over output practice.

**Metalanguage: less than you think, but not zero.** *Analysed knowledge*
(being able to identify and manipulate the pattern) correlates with proficiency
more strongly than knowledge of terminology.

**Grammar-translation.** There is no rigorous comparative literature condemning
it; the received wisdom is historical and ideological. Available comparisons
find it produces better discrete accuracy and worse spontaneous speaking than
communicative teaching, with most authors recommending hybridisation.

> **Design implications.** (a) Sequence by item properties (salience,
> regularity, frequency, semantic transparency), computable from a corpus. (b)
> Only a handful of structures need genuine gating; gate those on demonstrated
> mastery of the prerequisite stage. (c) Default to explicit rule statement
> plus structured input practice; do not rely on flooding or bolding. (d) Teach
> patterns via manipulation tasks, not terminology quizzes; introduce a term
> only when it makes a later rule shorter. (e) Retire the taboo on translation
> exercises; they are cheap to auto-grade and the evidence against them is
> absent.

## 2. Writing systems

**Component/radical decomposition is the best-supported principle.** Explicit
teaching of semantic and phonetic radicals improves orthographic awareness,
character recall, and the ability to infer meanings of new characters.

**Heisig's RTK has no controlled evidence base.** The component-plus-mnemonic
core is sound; the implementation is criticised for inventing keyword meanings,
deferring readings entirely (months with zero phonological access), and
teaching production before recognition-in-context.

**Phonological decoding is not optional.** Guan et al. (2011) compared
handwriting, reading-only, and pinyin-typing tutors: handwriting strengthened
orthographic representation and the character–meaning link but not the
character–phonology link; pinyin typing strengthened the phonology link.
Neither alone is sufficient.

**Handwriting is expensive and often not worth the time.** Lu, Ostrow &
Heffernan (2019, registered report) found learners who spent ~30% of practice
time on hand-copying scored significantly lower on recognition post-tests at
all delays, with no compensating gain on paper tests. Handwriting helps per
unit of exposure but loses on time-on-task. Mueller & Oppenheimer's
longhand-vs-laptop note-taking result does not transfer to character
acquisition.

**Mnemonics for syllabaries/alphabets.** Evidence is thin but positive. Kana
and Hangul are small, regular, and high-yield; the design question is
throughput.

> **Design implications.** (a) Teach components before characters, with a
> component graph driving ordering. (b) Never separate form from sound;
> introduce readings alongside the graph. (c) Make recognition the primary
> trained skill and typing the primary production skill; make handwriting an
> optional track flagged as costly. (d) Front-load kana/Hangul/Cyrillic in a
> compressed mnemonic-plus-SRS block (days, not weeks). (e) SRS is a scheduling
> technology, not a curriculum; ordering and decomposition do the pedagogical
> work.

## 3. Reading

**Extensive reading works, modestly.** Nakanishi (2015): pre/post *d* = 0.71;
vs control *d* = 0.46.

**Reading-while-listening beats reading alone.** Webb & Chang (2015), 61
learners reading and listening to 10 graded readers: relative vocabulary gains
44.1% immediately, 36.7% at three months.

**Glossing is one of the strongest, best-evidenced levers.** Yanagisawa, Webb &
Uchihara (2020), 359 effect sizes from 42 studies (N = 3,802): glossed reading
yielded 45.3% of unknown words learned immediately and 33.4% at delay, vs
26.6% / 19.8% unglossed. **Multiple-choice glosses were best**; glossaries and
in-text glosses were not reliably better than no gloss at delay. **L1 glosses
beat L2 glosses** by ~4–5 points, with no interaction with proficiency. Mode
made no significant difference.

**Coverage thresholds.** ~95% coverage = adequate comprehension with support
(~4,000–5,000 word families); ~98% = unassisted comprehension (~8,000
families). Kremmel et al.'s (2023) replication broadly upholds this while
questioning sharp threshold talk.

**Fluency is trainable and separately so.** Timed-reading and repeated-reading
interventions produce gains of ~13–50 wpm, retained at six weeks, with no
comprehension cost.

**Repetition counts.** ~8 encounters gets ~86% form recognition and ~75%
form–meaning recognition but only ~55% meaning recall. Narrow reading exploits
this.

> **Design implications.** (a) Build the reader around multiple-choice L1
> glosses on tap. Avoid in-text substitution and end-of-text glossaries. (b)
> Default every text to reading-while-listening with synchronised audio. (c)
> Compute per-text coverage against the learner's known-word model and route
> texts so the learner sits at ~95% with glossing, ~98% for speed work. (d)
> Ship a timed-reading fluency mode with wpm tracking and a ≥75% comprehension
> floor. (e) Schedule narrow reading sets and target ≥8 spaced encounters per
> new item; the Assimil parallel-text format is permanent interlinear glossing,
> better as a beginner scaffold than a steady state.

## 4. Writing

**Written corrective feedback works.** Kang & Han (2015), 21 studies: *d* = 0.68
on grammatical accuracy, moderated by proficiency, setting, and number of
sessions. Formally critiqued for study-quality heterogeneity.

**Focused feedback on narrow targets produces durable gains.** Bitchener &
Knoch (2010) found focused WCF on two article functions effective over ten
months.

**Dictogloss and dictation.** Yu, Boers & Tremblay (2025) found that retrieval
from memory, the defining feature of dictogloss versus plain dictation, is the
strongest predictor of learning outcomes.

**Translation.** Cook (2010) is an argument for reassessment, not an evidence
synthesis; the empirical case rests on the own-language literature (§6).

> **Design implications.** (a) Focused, rule-specific feedback on one or two
> targets at a time. (b) Prefer reconstruct-from-memory tasks over verbatim
> dictation. (c) L1→L2 translation is a legitimate, auto-checkable production
> exercise. (d) Journaling has no controlled effect-size literature; treat it
> as an engagement device.

## 5. Assessment and adaptivity

**CEFR is a descriptor framework, not a measurement scale, and cross-test
alignment is unreliable.** HSK's 1:1 mapping onto A1–C2 is widely rejected
(HSK 3 ≈ 600 words vs ~2,000 for B1); four published HSK 3.0→CEFR charts agree
on one level out of nine. JLPT tests only reading and listening.

**CAT/IRT is the mature technology.** The Duolingo English Test (2024
whitepaper) opens with 15–18 adaptively-selected yes/no vocabulary items,
scores adaptive sections by IRT ability estimates, exploits high inter-skill
correlation, and randomises some selection to prevent item over-exposure.
AutoIRT (arXiv:2409.08823) fits IRT parameters via AutoML on item features;
BanditCAT (arXiv:2410.21033) frames item selection as a bandit problem. Both
address cold start for auto-generated items.

**Knowledge tracing: no clear winner, and DKT misbehaves.** Deep Knowledge
Tracing has documented pathologies (predicted mastery dropping after a correct
answer, large swings), and simpler models (hierarchical/temporal IRT, BKT, Elo)
often match it. Elo is attractive for self-study: online, one-pass, jointly
estimates learner ability and item difficulty, no training corpus.

**Spacing should be learned, not fixed.** Settles & Meeder (2016): half-life
regression reduced recall-prediction error by >45% vs baselines.

**Optimal difficulty has a quantitative answer.** Wilson et al. (2019, *Nature
Communications*): for gradient-descent learners the optimal training error rate
is ~15.87%, i.e. **target ~85% accuracy**. A formal result for a specific
learner class, converging with desirable-difficulties intuitions.

**Self-assessment is moderately valid.** Li & Zhang (2021), 67 studies, N >
68,500: overall r = .466 with measured performance (listening .486, reading
.451, speaking .442, writing .381). Accuracy improves with can-do criteria and
training.

**Vocabulary size tests.** Nation's VST is standard; yes/no tests are fast but
response-bias-prone: Eyckmans (2004) found 13–25% false-alarm rates. Nonword
anchors and correction formulas are mandatory.

**Automatic speaking scoring is usable.** ETS SpeechRater agrees with human
raters at r ≈ .85 against human–human r ≈ .90. Delivery r = .784, overall
.686, fluency .618, pronunciation .606, content .574, grammar/vocabulary .499.

> **Design implications.** (a) Placement: adaptive yes/no vocabulary block
> with nonword anchors, followed by IRT-scored adaptive items. (b) Elo for
> online joint difficulty/ability estimation; avoid DKT-driven user-facing
> mastery displays. (c) Learn the spacing function from your own logs. (d)
> Target ~85% success on generated items. (e) Use can-do self-assessment for
> goal-setting and calibration, never as the placement signal. (f) Report
> internal levels; give CEFR/JLPT/HSK only as loose cross-references.

## 6. Transfer and cross-linguistic influence

**Typological distance predicts difficulty, coarsely.** FSI's categories
correlate with measured distance (WALS-syntax distance predicts transfer at r
≈ 0.78–0.83). Effects are domain-specific: close L1s help character and
vocabulary acquisition but can produce grammar interference at higher
proficiency.

**Cognates help; false friends hurt.** The cognate facilitation effect is
robust, scaled by orthographic overlap. False cognates are known worse than
non-cognate controls; they are a distinct, harder item class.

**The monolingual principle has no evidential foundation.** Hall & Cook (2012)
conclude the English-only assumption is ideological rather than empirically
grounded, and universally violated in practice. Cummins' interdependence model
has direct empirical support.

> **Design implications.** (a) Make L1 a first-class configuration parameter
> driving item difficulty priors, cognate mining, false-friend warning items,
> and script-transfer assumptions. (b) Front-load cognates for a fast early
> win; mine false friends into a separately-scheduled, higher-repetition,
> contrast-drilled class. (c) Use L1 explanations and L1 glosses without
> apology. (d) Set time-to-level expectations from FSI-style distance.

## 7. Genuine disagreements

1. **Does developmental readiness constrain teaching?** Pienemann says yes for
   stage-bound morphosyntax; the explicit-instruction meta-analyses suggest the
   constraint is narrow.

3. **Handwriting for characters.** Compatible only under a time-cost framing.
4. **Coverage thresholds as thresholds.** Cliff vs smooth function.

**Three caveats on evidence quality.** The strongest numbers here (glossing,
spacing, WCF) sit alongside topics with essentially no controlled evidence
(Heisig, journaling, kana mnemonics, translation-as-exercise). Almost the
entire instructed-SLA literature is classroom L2 English with short delayed
post-tests. Several of the strongest results come from publication-bias-flagged
literatures or vendor research.
