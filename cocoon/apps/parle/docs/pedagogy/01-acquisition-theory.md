# Core second language acquisition theory and what it implies for programme design

Part of the [Parle pedagogy survey](README.md). Effect sizes are reported as
found in the sources; where a figure could only be verified via a secondary
source this is noted.

## 1. Krashen's Monitor Model: what survived, what didn't

**The claims.** Krashen's five hypotheses: acquisition/learning (subconscious
acquisition and conscious learning are separate, non-interfacing systems);
Monitor (learned knowledge only edits output, never generates it); natural
order (morphemes acquired in a fixed sequence regardless of teaching order);
input (acquisition happens by comprehending input at *i+1*); affective filter
(anxiety and low motivation block input from reaching the acquisition device).

**What has held up.**

- **Input is necessary and its quantity matters enormously.** No serious
  researcher disputes this. Rod Ellis makes "extensive L2 input" one of his ten
  principles of instructed language learning, and Long's own position is that
  meaning-focused input is the backbone of a course.
- **There are developmental sequences** that resist instruction (Pienemann's
  teachability constraints; Ellis's "built-in syllabus" principle). This is the
  natural-order hypothesis in a defensible form.
- **Affect matters**, in the weak sense that anxiety correlates negatively with
  performance. The strong "filter" mechanism does not.

**What has not held up.**

- **Non-interface is rejected by most of the field.** Norris & Ortega (2000)
  found focused instruction produced large gains (mean *d* ≈ 0.96), with
  **explicit instruction (*d* ≈ 1.13) clearly outperforming implicit (*d* ≈
  0.54)** across 49 unique samples. That result is incompatible with "learning
  cannot become acquisition."
- ***i+1* is not operationalisable.** Neither *i* nor *+1* can be measured
  independently, which makes the input hypothesis unfalsifiable.
- **The affective filter is circular**: the only evidence of a raised filter is
  failure to acquire.
- **Input alone is insufficient.** Immersion research (the origin of Swain's
  work) showed learners with years of rich comprehensible input still
  fossilising on morphology and syntax.

**Design implications.** Build the programme on a very large volume of
comprehensible input, but treat "comprehensible input" as a necessary
condition, not a complete mechanism. Do not use Krashen to justify omitting
explicit explanation, corrective feedback, or production. Do use him to justify
making input the largest single component and keeping affective load low
(low-stakes practice, no forced public performance).

## 2. Interaction, output, noticing

**Long's Interaction Hypothesis.** Negotiation for meaning (clarification
requests, confirmation checks, recasts) makes input comprehensible and draws
attention to form at the moment of communicative need. Mackey & Goo's (2007)
meta-analysis of ~28 interaction studies found large effects for interaction,
notably **larger on delayed than immediate post-tests** (reported around *d* ≈
0.71 immediate, *d* ≈ 1.09 delayed for feedback in interaction; secondary-source
figures), and larger in foreign-language than second-language settings (*d* ≈
0.88 vs 0.64).

**Corrective feedback.** Li (2010), 33 studies including 11 dissertations, found
an **overall medium effect (*d* = 0.61; random effects 0.64)**. Explicit
feedback wins on immediate and short-delayed tests, but implicit feedback's
advantage grows over time. Lab studies beat classroom studies; short treatments
beat long ones, a warning that these effects are partly artefacts of
measurement proximity.

**Swain's Output Hypothesis.** Output serves three functions: noticing the gap
between intention and ability, hypothesis testing, and metalinguistic
reflection. Evidence for the noticing function is reasonably good in
small-scale studies; evidence that output directly drives grammatical
development is weaker than the interaction evidence. Output's strongest
empirical role is in **proceduralisation and fluency**, not initial acquisition.

**Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis.** In its strong form ("no learning without
awareness at the level of noticing") it is contested; statistical and implicit
learning research (Nick Ellis, Rebuschat) shows learning without reportable
awareness. In its weak form ("attention to form massively increases the chance
of uptake") it is one of the best-supported ideas in the field and underwrites
almost all form-focused pedagogy.

**Design implications.**

- Build in tasks that create communicative pressure and a reason to negotiate.
- Provide corrective feedback of **both kinds**: explicit correction for fast
  gains, recast-like implicit feedback for durability.
- Schedule output not as a test of input but as a gap-finder: have learners
  attempt production, then re-expose them to input containing the forms they
  failed on.
- Anything you want learned must be made salient. Silent exposure to a
  low-salience form (English third-person *-s*, Spanish subjunctive) rarely
  suffices.

## 3. Nation's Four Strands, the most directly actionable framework

Nation's claim: a well-balanced course gives roughly **equal time (25% each)**
to

1. **Meaning-focused input** (listening and reading for the message, with
   ≥95–98% known items),
2. **Meaning-focused output** (speaking and writing to convey a message),
3. **Language-focused learning** (deliberate study of grammar, vocabulary,
   pronunciation, strategies),
4. **Fluency development** (using only already-known language, under time
   pressure, to get faster).

Nation's rule of thumb: **no more than 25% on deliberate language study, and at
least 25% on fluency**. "If these strands are not equally represented, then the
design of the course needs to be looked at again."

Supporting numbers from Nation's vocabulary work:

- **98% lexical coverage** for unassisted comprehension; **95%** for minimally
  adequate comprehension (Hu & Nation 2000; confirmed by Laufer &
  Ravenhorst-Kalovski 2010, who tie 98% to ~8,000 word families and 95% to
  ~4,000–5,000).
- **8,000–9,000 word families** for unsimplified written text at 98%;
  **6,000–7,000** for unscripted speech (Nation 2006).
- Fluency-development activities must contain essentially **no unknown items**.

**Design implications.** A programme can be audited against this: log where
learner minutes go and check the four ratios. Most commercial products fail
this audit by putting 70–90% of time in language-focused learning (flashcards,
grammar drills) and ~0% in fluency development. Gate reading and listening
material by coverage, and build the vocabulary curriculum around frequency
bands with 8,000–9,000 families as the long-run target for reading.

## 4. Skill Acquisition Theory (DeKeyser)

Three stages: **declarative** (know the rule, use it slowly and consciously) →
**procedural** (rule compiled into a production, performance much faster) →
**automatised** (fast, accurate, low-effort, resistant to interference). The
declarative→procedural transition is a sharp early drop in reaction time;
automatisation is a long tail of tuning following a power law.

Two findings with hard design consequences:

- **Practice is skill-specific.** Learners trained receptively perform far
  worse when tested productively, and vice versa. The same declarative
  knowledge yields separate procedural skills. Comprehension practice does not
  automatically buy production ability.
- **Explicit knowledge is a useful springboard**, but only if followed by large
  amounts of meaningful, communicative practice. Rule knowledge that is never
  proceduralised stays a Monitor.

**Design implications.** Sequence: brief explicit presentation → controlled but
meaningful practice → progressively freer, time-pressured practice. Practise
each modality you care about separately. Expect the automatisation phase to
require far more repetitions than the initial learning. Kim & Webb's (2022)
meta-analysis of spaced practice (98 effect sizes, 48 experiments, N = 3,411)
supports distributing that practice, with a medium effect (*g* ≈ 0.58 on
immediate post-tests, per secondary report) for spaced over massed.

## 5. Usage-based and emergentist accounts, and chunks

Nick Ellis's **Associative-Cognitive CREED**: language learning is
Construction-based, Rational, Exemplar-based, Emergent and Dialectic, driven by
the same statistical learning mechanisms as other cognition. Grammar emerges
from the accumulation of form–meaning exemplars; **frequency, recency and
salience** determine what gets learned. This account explains adult limitations
via learned attention and blocking: L1-entrenched cues block attention to L2
cues, which is exactly why noticing and explicit instruction help.

**Frequency in practice.** Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019), 45 effect sizes
from 26 studies (N = 1,918): the correlation between number of encounters and
incidental vocabulary learning is **r = .34**, real but medium. Incidental
learning from reading alone is slow and requires many encounters.

**Formulaic language.** Corpus work suggests up to ~50% of native production is
formulaic. Wray (2002) notes that **literate adults tend to unpack chunks into
words rather than store them holistically**, the opposite of what the
chunk-based route requires. Granger (1998) found no straight line from prefabs
to creative language in learner corpora. Lewis's Lexical Approach ("language is
grammaticalised lexis, not lexicalised grammar") is theoretically
well-motivated but has thin experimental support as a method.

**Design implications.** Teach multi-word units as units (collocations,
sentence frames, discourse markers) and count them as vocabulary. Order content
by corpus frequency. Because *r* ≈ .34, deliberate vocabulary work is needed to
supplement incidental pickup, which is exactly Nation's language-focused strand.

## 6. Explicit vs implicit instruction: the meta-analytic record

| Study | Scope | Headline |
|---|---|---|
| Norris & Ortega (2000) | 49 unique samples, 1980–1998 | Focused instruction *d* ≈ 0.96; explicit 1.13 vs implicit 0.54; Focus-on-Form ≈ Focus-on-Forms; effects durable |
| Spada & Tomita (2010) | 41 studies | Explicit > implicit for **both** simple and complex features; explicit helps spontaneous use, not just controlled |
| Goo, Grañena, Yilmaz & Novella (2015) | 34 samples, 1999–2011 | Replicates the explicit advantage; explicit *g* ≈ 1.44 on free production; oral + written treatment combined works best |
| Shintani (2015) | 42 experiments, 33 studies | Processing Instruction > production-based for **receptive** knowledge; production-based equals or beats PI for **productive** knowledge |

**Caveat widely made against Norris & Ortega:** the explicit advantage is
inflated by outcome measures. Explicit groups are typically tested on discrete,
metalinguistic, untimed instruments that favour explicit knowledge. When free,
spontaneous production is the measure, the gap narrows, though Spada & Tomita
and Goo et al. suggest it does not vanish.

**Focus on Form vs Focus on Forms.** Long's distinction: *Focus on Form* is
brief, reactive attention to code features during a meaning-primary task;
*Focus on Forms* is a syllabus of isolated structures taught in a predetermined
order. Norris & Ortega found the two produced equivalent effects, an
uncomfortable result for task-based advocates.

**VanPatten's Processing Instruction.** Structured input activities that force
learners to attend to a form to get meaning, plus explicit information about
the processing strategy that misleads them. Shintani's meta-analysis is the
fairest verdict: PI is efficient for building comprehension-side knowledge, but
you cannot get production for free from it.

**Design implications.** Include explicit grammar explanation; it is cheap and
the evidence supports it. Keep it brief and tie it immediately to input and
use. Prefer reactive, just-in-time explanation ("pop-up grammar") over long
grammar units. If the programme aims at speaking, PI-style input activities
alone will not deliver it.

## 7. Task-Based Language Teaching

Bryfonski & McKay (2019), 52 studies, is the first meta-analysis of
programme-level TBLT: **overall *d* = 0.93** with positive stakeholder
perceptions. Xuan, Cheung & Liu's technical comment criticised loose inclusion
criteria, oversimplified aggregation and neglected moderators, and recomputed
**g = 0.61**. Boers & Faez (2023) argue the comparison conditions are too
heterogeneous to support any relative-effectiveness claim. Treat TBLT as
well-supported in principle and not yet proven superior to well-designed
alternatives.

**Ellis's ten principles of instructed language learning (2005)**, roughly:
(1) develop both formulaic expressions and rule-based competence; (2) focus
predominantly on meaning; (3) also focus on form; (4) target implicit
knowledge primarily, without neglecting explicit; (5) respect the learner's
built-in syllabus; (6) require extensive L2 input; (7) require opportunities
for output; (8) make interaction central; (9) account for individual
differences; (10) assess free production as well as controlled production.

**Design implications.** Structure activity around tasks with a real outcome:
pre-task (input, useful language), task, post-task (focus on form, repetition
for fluency). Principle 10 is the test most products fail: if the only
assessment is multiple-choice or discrete-item, you optimise for the wrong
knowledge type.

## 8. Extensive reading and listening

| Meta-analysis | Scope | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Nakanishi (2015), *TESOL Quarterly* | 34 studies, 43 ESs, N = 3,942 | Pre–post *d* = 0.71; vs control *d* = 0.46 |
| Jeon & Day (2016), *RFL* | 71 samples, 49 studies, N = 5,919 | Pre–post *d* = 0.79; vs control *d* = 0.57; larger for adults, EFL > ESL, web-based > paper |
| Krashen (2017) | 19 experimental studies | Reading comprehension mean ES 0.88; cloze 0.73 (author is a partisan) |
| 2025 *Educational Psychology Review* meta-analysis | 74 studies | Positive across all domains, small-to-medium; **effects larger when text choice was limited and accountability was built in** |

That last moderator finding is the most useful and the most counter-doctrinal:
pure free-choice, no-accountability extensive reading (the orthodox Day &
Bamford model) performed worse than reading with constrained choice and an
accountability mechanism.

**Extensive listening** has a much thinner evidence base (individual studies
rather than a solid meta-analysis). Krashen's **narrow listening and reading**
(staying within one topic, author or series so vocabulary and background
knowledge recycle) is well-motivated by coverage arithmetic.

**Design implications.** Make extensive reading and listening a core,
timetabled strand. Gate texts by 95–98% coverage; graded readers exist to make
that possible at low proficiency. Prefer narrow reading and listening for
recycling. Add light accountability (a log, a brief response, a comprehension
check). Offer curated choice rather than unlimited choice.

## 9. "Pure comprehensible input" movements

**ALG (J. Marvin Brown, AUA Bangkok, from 1984).** Claim: adults can acquire
like children if they take input only, and (the strong claim) any early
speaking, thinking about the language, or conscious study causes permanent
"damage" capping ultimate attainment. Brown's evidence is autobiographical and
anecdotal. There is **no controlled research testing the damage hypothesis**.
The claim is directly contradicted by the skill-acquisition and
explicit-instruction literatures.

**Postovsky (1974)** is the study repeatedly cited for delayed speaking: adults
learning Russian with four weeks of comprehension-only outperformed an
immediate-production group on pronunciation, listening and fluency. It is one
small, old study comparing a listening approach against 1970s audiolingual
drilling, and it supports a four-week delay, not the multi-hundred-hour silence
ALG advocates.

**Dreaming Spanish** (Pablo Román) is the popular operationalisation, with an
hours roadmap (commonly ~600 h before speaking, ~1,000–1,500 h to comfortable
conversation). Its user-reported outcome data are self-selected and
self-reported; there is no peer-reviewed evaluation.

**TPRS (Blaine Ray).** Comprehensible input through co-created stories with
heavy repetition. Published studies are mostly small quasi-experiments,
typically favourable on speaking and affect. TPRS is not a silent-period method;
it demands constant learner response.

**Design implications.** Adopt the defensible core: massive comprehensible
input, no forced early production, tolerance of a personal silent period, low
anxiety. Reject the damage hypothesis. If the programme wants speaking, it must
eventually schedule speaking; the skill-specificity finding (§4) is decisive.

## 10. Time on task

**FSI (US Foreign Service Institute)** hours to ILR Speaking-3 / Reading-3
(roughly CEFR B2–C1) for motivated adult native English speakers in intensive
full-time classes:

- **Category I** ~24–30 weeks, **600–750 h** (Spanish, French, Italian,
  Portuguese, Dutch, Swedish)
- **Category II** ~36 weeks, **900 h** (German, Indonesian, Swahili, Haitian
  Creole)
- **Category III** ~44 weeks, **1,100 h** (Russian, Hindi, Greek, Hebrew,
  Polish, Thai, Turkish, Vietnamese)
- **Category IV** ~88 weeks, **2,200 h** (Arabic, Mandarin, Cantonese,
  Japanese, Korean)

These are classroom hours only, exclude self-study, and describe selected
learners.

**Cambridge English guided learning hours** (cumulative from zero, motivated
adults): A1 ~90–100 h; A2 ~180–200 h; B1 ~350–400 h; B2 ~500–600 h; C1
~700–800 h; C2 ~1,000–1,200 h. Roughly **100–200 h per level transition**.

**Design implications.** These numbers set honest expectations and let a
programme convert a learner's goal into a time budget. Combined with Nation's
25% rule: a B2 Spanish goal ≈ 500–600 h ⇒ ~125–150 h each of input, output,
language-focused study and fluency work.

## Points of genuine disagreement

1. **Interface question.** Non-interface (Krashen), weak interface (R. Ellis),
   strong interface (DeKeyser). The field's centre of gravity is
   weak-to-strong, but this is unresolved.
2. **Does the explicit advantage survive better measures?** Goo et al.'s
   free-production result (*g* ≈ 1.44) is the strongest counter to the
   measurement critique.
3. **Is TBLT actually better?** *d* = 0.93 vs *g* = 0.61 vs "not yet
   answerable".
4. **Does output cause acquisition or only reveal gaps?**
5. **Delayed speaking.** Almost no controlled evidence beyond Postovsky.
6. **Chunks as a route into grammar.** Lewis and Nick Ellis say yes; Wray and
   Granger say adult literate learners don't take that route.
7. **Extensive reading orthodoxy.** Free choice and no accountability vs the
   2025 finding that constrained choice plus accountability produce larger
   effects.
